Where the chapter starts

Someone at home has acidity after overeating. What do you give them?

Lemon juice, vinegar, or baking soda solution — only one of these actually helps. Before you read ahead, pick one in your head.

🍋

Lemon juice

Sour. It already contains citric acid. Adding acid to an acid problem makes it worse.

🥣

Vinegar

Also sour — acetic acid. Same mistake, different bottle.

✔️

Baking soda solution

Basic in nature. It cancels the extra acid in the stomach. This is the correct answer.

The one idea that runs through the whole chapter

An acid and a base cancel each other out.

Every single reaction in this chapter is a variation of that sentence. Acids are sour and turn blue litmus red. Bases are bitter and turn red litmus blue. Put them together and both disappear, leaving salt and water behind.

SourTaste of an acid — curd, lemon, tamarind
BitterTaste of a base — and it feels soapy
NeverTaste or touch a lab acid or base to test it. Use an indicator

2.1 · Indicators

Chemicals that tell on acids and bases

An indicator is a dye that changes colour depending on what it is dipped into. It is how we test a substance without tasting it.

Blue litmusstays blue
Red litmusstays red
Phenolphthaleincolourless
Methyl orangeorange

Tap a button. The tubes turn as they are dipped.

How to remember all four colours

“Acid Bites Blue · Base Bites Red.”

Whatever litmus you dip, the acid drags it to red and the base drags it to blue. So the final colour itself is the answer: red = acid, blue = base.

For the other two: Phenolphthalein turns Pink in a base (P → P), and methyl orange goes red when angry — angry means acid.

🌿

Natural indicators

Litmus is a purple dye extracted from lichen, a plant of the division Thallophyta. In a neutral solution it stays purple. Turmeric is another natural indicator — a curry stain on white cloth turns reddish-brown when basic soap is scrubbed on it, and goes yellow again on washing with plenty of water.

Red cabbage leaves, coloured petals of Hydrangea, Petunia and Geranium also work as acid–base indicators.

👃

Olfactory indicators

Some substances change their smell in acidic or basic media. These are called olfactory indicators — useful whenever a colour change cannot be seen.

  • Onion — the smell vanishes in a base, stays in an acid
  • Vanilla essence — smell disappears in a base
  • Clove oil — behaves the same way
Shortcut

Nose test = O V C

Onion, Vanilla, Clove. Bases kill the smell; acids keep it.

Synthetic indicators used in the lab — methyl orange · phenolphthalein

2.1.2 · Reaction with metals

Drop a metal into an acid and you get a pop

Zinc granules in dilute sulphuric acid start fizzing. Pass that gas through soap solution, bring a burning candle near a gas-filled bubble, and it burns with a sharp pop sound. That pop is the signature of hydrogen.

H2 · pop!
Zinc granules in dilute H2SO4
The rule
Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas
Zn (s) + H2SO4 (dil.) → ZnSO4 (aq) + H2 (g)
Zn (s) + 2HCl (dil.) → ZnCl2 (aq) + H2 (g)

The same thing happens with HNO3 and CH3COOH. The metal simply pushes the hydrogen out of the acid and takes its place — what is left behind is a salt.

Remember it like this

M A S H — Metal + Acid gives Salt + Hydrogen.

Say “MASH” once and the whole equation is in order: the two things you mix (M, A) and the two things you get (S, H).

How to test that the gas is hydrogen

  • Bubbles appear on the surface of the zinc granules
  • Pass the gas through soap solution — bubbles form because the gas is trapped in soapy film
  • Bring a burning candle near a gas-filled bubble
  • It burns with a pop sound — that confirms hydrogen

Bases with metals — the odd case

Zinc also reacts with a base. Sodium hydroxide gives hydrogen gas again, plus a salt called sodium zincate. Such reactions are not possible with all metals.

2NaOH (aq) + Zn (s) → Na2ZnO2 (s) + H2 (g)

Na2ZnO2 is sodium zincate.

Remember it like this

Zinc is the show-off — it reacts with both sides.

Acid or base, zinc still releases H2. And the product keeps zinc's own name inside it: sodium zincate.

2.1.3 · Carbonates and hydrogencarbonates

Add acid to a carbonate and it fizzes like a cold drink

Sodium carbonate and sodium hydrogencarbonate both effervesce when dilute HCl is poured on them. The gas coming out is carbon dioxide, and there is a beautiful two-step test for it.

The rule
Metal carbonate / Metal hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water
Na2CO3 (s) + 2HCl (aq) → 2NaCl (aq) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g)
NaHCO3 (s) + HCl (aq) → NaCl (aq) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g)

Limestone, chalk and marble are all different forms of the same compound — calcium carbonate, CaCO3.

Remember it like this

Carbonate + acid = a shaken soda bottle.

Salt stays in the glass, the gas escapes, a little water is left. Products in order: Salt + CO₂ + Water. If you can picture the fizz, you can never forget the CO2.

Clear lime water

The lime water test

Lime water is calcium hydroxide solution. Pass the CO2 through it and it turns milky — that white precipitate is calcium carbonate.

Ca(OH)2 (aq) + CO2 (g) → CaCO3 (s) + H2O (l)

lime water  →  white precipitate

Keep passing excess CO2 and the milkiness disappears again, because the insoluble carbonate turns into a soluble hydrogencarbonate.

CaCO3 (s) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g) → Ca(HCO3)2 (aq)

soluble in water — so the solution goes clear again

Remember it like this

Milky, then clear. Too much gas dissolves the milk.

A little CO2 makes a solid you can see. Excess CO2 makes it soluble and it vanishes. CaCO₃ = milk, Ca(HCO₃)₂ = clear.

2.1.4 · Acids and bases with each other

Two enemies meet and both disappear

Take dilute NaOH with a few drops of phenolphthalein and it turns pink. Add dilute HCl drop by drop and the pink fades away — because the base is no longer a base. Add NaOH again and the pink comes back.

H+from the acid
OH−from the base
H2Owater, and nothing else

Scroll slowly — the two ions turn, meet, and vanish into a water molecule.

Neutralisation reaction
Base + Acid → Salt + Water
NaOH (aq) + HCl (aq) → NaCl (aq) + H2O (l)

The effect of a base is nullified by an acid and vice-versa. This is the reaction that explains the antacid you take for acidity.

Remember it like this

“A Boy Sells Water” — Acid + Base → Salt + Water.

Four words, four chemicals, same order every time. Whenever a question says neutralisation, write Salt + Water on the right side before you think about anything else.

2.1.5 & 2.1.6 · Oxides

Metal oxides behave like bases. Non-metal oxides behave like acids.

Add dilute hydrochloric acid slowly to black copper oxide and the powder dissolves, leaving a blue-green solution. That colour is copper(II) chloride.

Metal oxide — a basic oxide
Metal oxide + Acid → Salt + Water
CuO (s) + 2HCl (aq) → CuCl2 (aq) + H2O (l)

Black copper oxide dissolves; the blue-green colour is copper(II) chloride. Because metallic oxides react with acids to give salt and water — exactly like a base does — they are called basic oxides.

Non-metal oxide — an acidic oxide
Non-metallic oxide + Base → Salt + Water
Ca(OH)2 (aq) + CO2 (g) → CaCO3 (s) + H2O (l)

Calcium hydroxide is a base and carbon dioxide is a non-metallic oxide. Since this is just like a base reacting with an acid, non-metallic oxides are acidic in nature.

Remember it like this

Opposites attract.

Metal oxide is basic, so it needs an acid to react. Non-metal oxide is acidic, so it needs a base. Both give the same pair of products: Salt + Water.

Quick check: metals sit on the left of the periodic table and give bases; non-metals sit on the right and give acids.

2.2 · What do all acids and all bases have in common?

Every acid is hiding the same ion

Fix two nails in a cork, connect them to a 6 volt battery through a bulb and a switch, and dip them in different solutions. Dilute HCl lights the bulb. Glucose and alcohol solutions do not.

💡

The bulb glows — so current is flowing

Glowing means there is a flow of electric current through the solution, and that current is carried by ions. Glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen too, but their solutions do not conduct — so they are not acids.

Acids contain H+ as the cation, along with an anion: Cl− in HCl, NO3− in HNO3, SO42− in H2SO4, CH3COO− in CH3COOH. The common cation is always H+.

💧

No water, no acid

Dry HCl gas does not change the colour of dry blue litmus paper. Moist litmus paper turns red. So the H+ ion cannot separate from the HCl molecule unless water is present.

HCl + H2O → H3O+ + Cl−
H+ + H2O → H3O+

Hydrogen ions cannot exist alone. They always attach to a water molecule, so we write them as H+(aq) or as the hydronium ion H3O+.

Remember it like this

H+ never travels alone — it always holds water's hand.

That handshake is the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺. It also explains the exam favourite: dry HCl gas has no effect on dry litmus, because without water there is no H+.

And every base is hiding OH−

NaOH (s) —H2O→ Na+ (aq) + OH− (aq)
KOH (s) —H2O→ K+ (aq) + OH− (aq)
Mg(OH)2 (s) —H2O→ Mg2+ (aq) + 2OH− (aq)

Bases generate hydroxide ions in water. Bases that dissolve in water are called alkalis — and all bases do not dissolve in water.

Alkali ≠ base

Every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali. Alkalis are soapy to touch, bitter and corrosive. Never taste or touch them — they can cause harm.

Neutralisation, seen as ions

Acid + Base → Salt + Water
HX + MOH → MX + HOH
H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O (l)

Mixing with water

The rule that prevents burns

Dissolving an acid or a base in water is a highly exothermic process. Concentrated nitric acid or sulphuric acid must always be added slowly to water, with constant stirring.

⚠️

If you add water to concentrated acid

  • The heat generated may make the mixture splash out
  • It can cause burns
  • The glass container may break because of excessive local heating

This is why concentrated acid and sodium hydroxide bottles carry a corrosive warning sign.

💉

What dilution actually does

Mixing an acid or base with water lowers the concentration of ions — H3O+ or OH− — per unit volume. That process is called dilution, and we say the acid or the base has been diluted.

Remember it like this

A A A — Always Add Acid (to water, never the reverse).

One more way to hold it: “Do what you oughta, add acid to water.” If you ever forget which goes into which, remember that water is the bigger, calmer partner — the acid goes into it, slowly.

2.3 · How strong are acid or base solutions?

The pH scale — a number for how sour or how soapy

Indicators tell us whether something is an acid or a base. The pH scale tells us how much. It measures the hydrogen ion concentration in a solution, from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very alkaline).

7
Pure water
Neutral
0 · very acidic14 · very alkaline

Where the name comes from

The p in pH stands for potenz, the German word for power. The scale was developed to measure hydrogen ion concentration in a solution. A universal indicator — a mixture of several indicators — shows different colours at different hydrogen ion concentrations, and paper impregnated with it is what we use to measure pH.

Reading the scale

  • pH of a neutral solution is exactly 7
  • Below 7 → acidic. The lower the number, the more acidic the solution
  • From 7 to 14 → OH− concentration increases, so the alkali gets stronger
  • Higher hydronium ion concentration means lower pH value
Remember it like this

Lower the number, louder the sour.

More H+ ions crowded in the solution → smaller pH. And 7 sits in the middle like a referee: everything on its left is an acid, everything on its right is a base.

For the German word: p for potenz = power. Both start with p.

Strong acid or weak acid?

The strength depends on the number of H+ ions produced. Take hydrochloric acid and acetic acid of the same concentration — say one molar — and they still produce different amounts of hydrogen ions.

  • Acids that give more H+ ions → strong acids
  • Acids that give fewer H+ ions → weak acids
  • Bases follow the same logic with OH− ions
🌌

Acids on other planets

The atmosphere of Venus is made up of thick white and yellowish clouds of sulphuric acid. Do you think life can exist on that planet?

2.3.1 · Importance of pH in everyday life

Your body, your food, the rain and your teeth all run on pH

🫀

Are plants and animals pH sensitive?

Our body works within the narrow pH range of 7.0 to 7.8. Living organisms can survive only in a narrow range of pH change.

When the pH of rain water is less than 5.6, it is called acid rain. When acid rain flows into rivers it lowers the pH of the river water, and survival of aquatic life in such rivers becomes difficult.

🌱

The pH of the soil in your backyard

Plants require a specific pH range for healthy growth. To find the pH a plant needs, collect soil from various places and check its pH — and note down which plants are already growing in the region that soil came from.

🦠

pH in our digestive system

Our stomach produces hydrochloric acid. It helps in digesting food without harming the stomach. During indigestion the stomach produces too much acid, which causes pain and irritation.

To get rid of this pain, people use bases called antacids. These neutralise the excess acid. Magnesium hydroxide — milk of magnesia — is a mild base often used for this purpose.

🦷

pH change as the cause of tooth decay

Tooth decay starts when the pH of the mouth is lower than 5.5. Tooth enamel is made of calcium hydroxyapatite, a crystalline form of calcium phosphate, and is the hardest substance in the body. It does not dissolve in water, but it is corroded when the pH in the mouth falls below 5.5.

Bacteria in the mouth produce acids by degrading the sugar and food particles left after eating. The best way to prevent this is to clean the mouth after eating. Toothpastes, which are generally basic, neutralise the excess acid and prevent tooth decay.

Two numbers that students always swap

Teeth 5.5 · Rain 5.6

Inside is smaller. What is inside your mouth takes the smaller number (5.5), what falls from outside takes the bigger one (5.6). Both are just below the middle of the acidic side — and both mean trouble.

Self defence by animals and plants through chemical warfare

🐝 Bee sting

A bee sting leaves an acid in the skin, which causes pain and irritation. Rubbing a mild base like baking soda on the stung area gives relief.

🌿 Nettle sting

Nettle is a herbaceous plant that grows in the wild. Its leaves have stinging hair which inject methanoic acid, causing burning pain. The traditional remedy is rubbing the area with the leaf of the dock plant, which often grows beside the nettle.

Remember it like this

Sting is acid → cure is base.

Nature even keeps the cure next door: the dock plant grows right beside the nettle. Acid problem, basic solution — the same idea as the antacid in your stomach.

2.4 · More about salts

Every salt has two parents — an acid and a base

A salt is what is left after neutralisation. So you can always read a salt backwards to find the acid and the base it came from.

👪

2.4.1  Family of salts

Salts having the same positive or the same negative radical are said to belong to one family.

  • NaCl and Na2SO4 → family of sodium salts (same positive radical)
  • NaCl and KCl → family of chloride salts (same negative radical)

Other salts you should be able to write: potassium sulphate K2SO4, calcium sulphate CaSO4, magnesium sulphate MgSO4, copper sulphate CuSO4, sodium nitrate NaNO3, sodium carbonate Na2CO3, ammonium chloride NH4Cl.

Remember it like this

Same surname, same family.

Share the sodium → sodium family. Share the chloride → chloride family. One salt can belong to two families at once, just like you belong to your mother's and your father's.

📐

2.4.2  pH of salts

  • Strong acid + strong base → neutral salt, pH = 7
  • Strong acid + weak base → acidic salt, pH < 7
  • Strong base + weak acid → basic salt, pH > 7
Remember it like this

The stronger parent decides the child.

If the acid parent is stronger, the salt turns out acidic. If the base parent is stronger, the salt turns out basic. If both are equally strong, nobody wins — the salt is neutral.

Example: NaCl comes from strong HCl and strong NaOH, so it is neutral. Na₂CO₃ comes from strong NaOH and weak carbonic acid, so it is basic.

2.4.3 · Chemicals from common salt

One salt, and an entire industry comes out of it

Sodium chloride is formed by the combination of hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solution — a strong acid and a strong base — so it is a neutral salt. It is also the raw material for sodium hydroxide, baking soda, washing soda and bleaching powder.

🏖️

Where common salt comes from

Seawater contains many salts dissolved in it, and sodium chloride is separated from these. Deposits of solid salt are also found in several parts of the world. These large crystals are often brown due to impurities — this is called rock salt. Beds of rock salt were formed when seas of bygone ages dried up. Rock salt is mined like coal.

You must have heard of Mahatma Gandhi's Dandi March — sodium chloride was such an important symbol in our struggle for freedom.

⚡

The chlor-alkali process

When electricity is passed through an aqueous solution of sodium chloride, called brine, it decomposes to form sodium hydroxide.

The name comes straight from the products: chlor for chlorine, alkali for sodium hydroxide.

Anode → Cl2
Cathode → H2

The electrolysis cell turns as you scroll. Green bubbles are chlorine, blue are hydrogen.

Electrolysis of brine
2NaCl (aq) + 2H2O (l) → 2NaOH (aq) + Cl2 (g) + H2 (g)

Chlorine gas is given off at the anode, hydrogen gas at the cathode, and sodium hydroxide solution is formed near the cathode. All three products are useful.

Remember it like this

Anions go to the Anode.

Cl− is an anion, so chlorine appears at the anode. Whatever is left over — hydrogen — must come out at the cathode, and the sodium hydroxide stays behind in the solution near it.

And the name of the process lists two of the three products for you: chlorine + alkali. Hydrogen is the free bonus.

Cl2 — chlorineWater treatment, swimming pools, PVC, disinfectants, CFCs, pesticides
H2 — hydrogenFuels, margarine, and ammonia for fertilisers
NaOH — sodium hydroxideDe-greasing metals, soaps and detergents, paper making, artificial fibres

Three famous chemicals from common salt

Bleaching powder, baking soda, washing soda

1  Bleaching powder — CaOCl2

The chlorine produced during the electrolysis of brine is used to manufacture bleaching powder. It is produced by the action of chlorine on dry slaked lime, Ca(OH)2. It is represented as CaOCl2, though the actual composition is quite complex.

2Ca(OH)2 + 2Cl2 → Ca(ClO)2 + CaCl2 + 2H2O

Bleaching powder is used

  • For bleaching cotton and linen in the textile industry, bleaching wood pulp in paper factories, and bleaching washed clothes in laundry
  • As an oxidising agent in many chemical industries
  • To make drinking water free from germs
Remember it like this

Slaked lime takes a chlorine shower.

Formula reads left to right exactly as its parents: Ca from lime, O from the hydroxide, Cl2 from chlorine → CaOCl₂. For the uses: Bleach clothes, bleach paper, kill germs.

2  Baking soda — NaHCO3

Commonly used in the kitchen for making tasty crispy pakoras, and sometimes added for faster cooking. Its chemical name is sodium hydrogencarbonate, and it is produced using sodium chloride as one of the raw materials.

How it is made
NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 → NH4Cl + NaHCO3

ammonium chloride  +  sodium hydrogencarbonate

It is a mild, non-corrosive basic salt — which is why it can neutralise an acid. On heating during cooking:

2NaHCO3 —Heat→ Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2

sodium hydrogencarbonate → sodium carbonate

Uses of baking soda

  • Baking powder is a mixture of baking soda and a mild edible acid such as tartaric acid. When heated or mixed with water: NaHCO3 + H+ → CO2 + H2O + sodium salt of acid. The carbon dioxide produced makes the bread or cake rise, turning it soft and spongy
  • An ingredient in antacids. Being alkaline, it neutralises excess acid in the stomach and provides relief
  • Used in soda-acid fire extinguishers
Remember it like this

The cake rises because a gas is trying to escape.

Every use of baking soda is really one idea: it releases CO2 and it kills acid. Gas → spongy cake and fire extinguisher. Base → antacid.

3  Washing soda — Na2CO3·10H2O

Another chemical obtained from sodium chloride. Sodium carbonate is made by heating baking soda; recrystallisation of sodium carbonate gives washing soda. It is also a basic salt.

Na2CO3 + 10H2O → Na2CO3·10H2O

sodium carbonate → washing soda

Uses of washing soda

  • In glass, soap and paper industries
  • In the manufacture of sodium compounds such as borax
  • As a cleaning agent for domestic purposes
  • For removing permanent hardness of water
Remember it like this

Baking soda has an H. Washing soda has two Na and ten waters.

NaHCO₃ vs Na₂CO₃·10H₂O. Baking happens in Heat, so baking soda keeps the H. Washing needs water, so washing soda carries ten of them.

For the four uses: G S P — Glass, Soap, Paper — plus borax, cleaning, and killing hardness of water.

2.4.4 · Are the crystals of salts really dry?

Some salts carry water inside the crystal

Heat a few blue crystals of copper sulphate in a dry boiling tube. Water droplets appear on the walls of the tube and the salt turns white. Add two or three drops of water back and the blue colour returns.

Blue crystals — CuSO4·5H2O

Copper sulphate crystals which seem to be dry contain water of crystallisation. When we heat the crystals, this water is removed and the salt turns white. If you moisten the crystals again with water, the blue colour reappears.

Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt. Five water molecules are present in one formula unit of copper sulphate.

CuSO4·5H2O hydrated copper sulphate — blue
CaSO4·2H2O gypsum

This is also the answer to whether Na2CO3·10H2O is wet — it is not. Those ten water molecules are locked inside the crystal, not sitting on it.

Plaster of Paris — CaSO4·½H2O

On heating gypsum at 373 K, it loses water molecules and becomes calcium sulphate hemihydrate. This is Plaster of Paris, the substance doctors use as a plaster for supporting fractured bones in the right position. It is a white powder, and on mixing with water it changes back to gypsum, giving a hard solid mass.

CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O

Plaster of Paris  +  water  →  gypsum

Why only half a water molecule? Because two formula units of CaSO4 share one molecule of water between them. Plaster of Paris is also used for making toys, materials for decoration, and for making surfaces smooth.

Remember it like this

Five, two, half — the water ladder.

CuSO₄·5H₂O blue copper sulphate → CaSO₄·2H₂O gypsum → CaSO₄·½H₂O Plaster of Paris. The numbers only ever go 5, 2, ½.

For the half: picture two CaSO4 units sharing one water bottle — half each. And 373 K is simply 100 °C, the temperature at which water leaves.

Every question in the chapter

Answered in small steps, with a way to remember each one

Read the question, cover the answer with your hand, and try it yourself first. Then check the gold box — that is the part you carry into the exam hall.

Set 1 · after indicators

1

Three test tubes contain distilled water, an acidic solution and a basic solution. You are given only red litmus paper. How will you identify the contents of each?

Answer in three moves

  • Dip red litmus paper into all three. Only one turns it blue — that tube is the base
  • Now you own a blue litmus paper. Dip that blue paper into the remaining two tubes
  • The one that turns it back to red is the acid. The one that does nothing is distilled water
How to remember this answer

Make your own blue paper first.

Red litmus can only catch a base. So catch the base, and let the base manufacture the blue paper you were not given. Then blue catches the acid, and water is whatever is left.

Set 2 · after reactions of acids and bases

1

Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass and copper vessels?

Answer

  • Curd and sour substances contain acids
  • These acids react with the metal of the brass or copper vessel
  • The reaction forms harmful metal salts (and hydrogen), which spoil the food and can cause food poisoning
How to remember this answer

Acid + Metal = MASH. And you should not eat the S.

2

Which gas is usually liberated when an acid reacts with a metal? Illustrate with an example. How will you test for the presence of this gas?

Answer in three parts

  • Gas: hydrogen, H2
  • Example: Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2
  • Test: pass the gas through soap solution and bring a burning candle near a gas-filled bubble — it burns with a pop sound
3

Metal compound A reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce effervescence. The gas evolved extinguishes a burning candle. Write a balanced equation if one of the compounds formed is calcium chloride.

Answer by clues

  • Gas extinguishes a burning candle → the gas is CO2 (hydrogen would have popped, not put it out)
  • CO2 with an acid comes from a carbonate
  • One product is calcium chloride → the metal is calcium
  • So compound A is calcium carbonate, CaCO3
CaCO3 (s) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl2 (aq) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g)
How to remember this answer

Pop = hydrogen. Put out = carbon dioxide.

That one line decides half the gas questions in this chapter. A gas that burns is H2; a gas that stops burning is CO2.

Set 3 · after ions, water and dilution

1

Why do HCl and HNO3 show acidic character in aqueous solution, while alcohol and glucose solutions do not?

Answer

  • HCl and HNO3 dissociate in water to give H+(aq) ions
  • H+(aq) ions are what make a solution acidic
  • Alcohol and glucose also contain hydrogen, but they do not release it as H+ ions in solution
  • No H+ ions → no acidic character, and no conduction of electricity either
How to remember this answer

Having hydrogen is not enough — it must be set free.

Glucose keeps its hydrogen locked up. HCl lets it go. Free H+ is the whole definition of an acid.

2

Why does an aqueous solution of an acid conduct electricity?

Answer

  • In water, an acid produces free ions — H3O+ (or H+(aq)) and the anion
  • Moving ions carry the electric current through the solution, so the bulb glows
3

Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry litmus paper?

Answer

  • Dry HCl gas has no water available
  • Without water, HCl cannot separate into H+ ions
  • No H+ ions means it does not behave as an acid, so dry litmus stays unchanged. Moist litmus paper does turn red
How to remember this answer

No water, no acid.

4

While diluting an acid, why must the acid be added to water and not water to the acid?

Answer

  • The process is highly exothermic — a lot of heat is released
  • If water is added to concentrated acid, the heat generated may make the mixture splash out and cause burns
  • The glass container may also break due to excessive local heating
  • Adding acid slowly to water, with constant stirring, spreads that heat safely
How to remember this answer

A A A — Always Add Acid.

5

How is the concentration of hydronium ions (H3O+) affected when a solution of an acid is diluted?

Answer

  • It decreases — the same number of ions is now spread through more volume
6

How is the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH−) affected when excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide?

Answer

  • It increases — more base means more OH− ions in the same solution
How to remember both questions above

Add water → ions go down. Add more base → ions go up.

Dilution spreads the ions thin. Adding more of the substance crowds them in. Nothing more to it.

Set 4 · after the pH scale

1

Solution A has pH 6 and solution B has pH 8. Which has more hydrogen ion concentration? Which is acidic and which is basic?

Answer

  • A has more hydrogen ion concentration, because lower pH means more H+
  • A is acidic (pH 6, below 7)
  • B is basic (pH 8, above 7)
2

What effect does the concentration of H+(aq) ions have on the nature of the solution?

Answer

  • More H+(aq) ions → the solution is more acidic and its pH is lower
  • Fewer H+(aq) ions → the solution is more basic and its pH is higher
3

Do basic solutions also have H+(aq) ions? If yes, then why are these basic?

Answer

  • Yes — basic solutions do contain H+(aq) ions
  • But the concentration of OH− ions is greater than the concentration of H+ ions
  • Whichever ion is in excess decides the nature, so the solution is basic
How to remember this answer

Both ions are always present. The majority wins the election.

4

Under what soil condition would a farmer treat his fields with quick lime (calcium oxide), slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) or chalk (calcium carbonate)?

Answer

  • When the soil is too acidic — that is, its pH is too low for the crop
  • All three of these are basic compounds, so they neutralise the excess acid and bring the soil pH back into a healthy range
How to remember this answer

Sick soil is sour. The cure is always a base.

What will happen if a solution of sodium hydrogencarbonate is heated? Give the equation of the reaction involved.

Answer

  • It decomposes to give sodium carbonate, water and carbon dioxide
2NaHCO3 —Heat→ Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2
How to remember this answer

Heat squeezes water and gas out of baking soda.

What is left behind is washing soda's parent, Na₂CO₃. This is exactly why cakes rise.

5

Write an equation to show the reaction between Plaster of Paris and water.

Answer

CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O

Plaster of Paris takes water back and becomes gypsum, setting into a hard solid mass.

How to remember this answer

½ + 1½ = 2. The water always adds up to gypsum.

Revision

What you have learnt

  • Acid-base indicators are dyes or mixtures of dyes used to indicate the presence of acids and bases.
  • Acidic nature is due to the formation of H+(aq) ions in solution; basic nature is due to the formation of OH−(aq) ions.
  • When an acid reacts with a metal, hydrogen gas is evolved and a corresponding salt is formed.
  • When a base reacts with a metal, hydrogen gas is evolved along with a salt whose negative ion is made of the metal and oxygen.
  • When an acid reacts with a metal carbonate or hydrogencarbonate, it gives the corresponding salt, carbon dioxide gas and water.
  • Acidic and basic solutions in water conduct electricity, because they produce hydrogen and hydroxide ions respectively.
  • Neutralisation of an acid by a base gives a salt and water: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l).
  • Metallic oxides are basic in nature; non-metallic oxides are acidic in nature.
  • The pH scale runs from 0 to 14 and measures hydrogen ion concentration. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic.
  • pH decides everyday things: acid rain below 5.6, tooth decay below 5.5, our body's range of 7.0 to 7.8, and the antacid you take for indigestion.
  • Salts of strong acids and strong bases are neutral; a strong acid with a weak base gives an acidic salt; a strong base with a weak acid gives a basic salt.
  • Common salt is the raw material for sodium hydroxide, bleaching powder, baking soda and washing soda.
  • Chlor-alkali: electricity through brine gives NaOH, chlorine at the anode and hydrogen at the cathode.
  • Salts may contain water of crystallisation — CuSO4·5H2O, gypsum CaSO4·2H2O, and Plaster of Paris CaSO4·½H2O.
